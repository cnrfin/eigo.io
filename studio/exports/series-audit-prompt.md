# Series audit brief: Great Britain (Beginner, Intermediate, Advanced)

Paste this whole brief into a new chat with another model (ChatGPT, Gemini, etc.), then attach the three course files:

- `great-britain-for-beginners.md` (A2)
- `great-britain-intermediate.md` (B1 → B2)
- `great-britain-advanced.md` (B2 → C1)

If the model can't take all three at once, run the brief three times, one course per chat. Each time, also attach the plan files (`great-britain-intermediate-plan.md`, `great-britain-advanced-plan.md`) so it can check continuity with the other courses.

---

You are reviewing a three-course English series before it is published. Be a strict, independent reviewer: a teacher-trainer, an editor and a fact-checker in one. Don't rewrite the courses. Find problems and explain them clearly.

## About the series

- **Learners:** adult Japanese learners in 1:1 online lessons with a British teacher. The slides sit beside the video call. Lessons are about 30–45 minutes.
- **Structure:** each course has 12 lessons in 4 units of 3. The last lesson of each unit has a progress check, and the last lesson of each course has a final check.
- **Levels:**

  | Course | Level | Story |
  |---|---|---|
  | Great Britain for Beginners | A2 | Haruka, 26, from Osaka, spends her first year in London: her flat, her landlady Gwen, her flatmate Tom, work with Priya, British food, manners and travel. |
  | Great Britain: Intermediate | B1 → B2 | Year two. A new colleague, Yuki, arrives. Practical life in the UK (renting, the NHS, the Post Office, travel problems, a visa). A light mystery: who is drawing the web comic *Milk Second* about Haruka's life? It turns out to be her mum. |
  | Great Britain: Advanced | B2 → C1 | Year three. Haruka is team lead. A new designer, Josh, from Sydney, clashes with her communication style. The team pitches to rebrand Hartley's Tea, decodes a very indirect British client, and solves a puzzle in the company archive (a crane on an old tin, and a 1920s friendship with a tea family in Uji). The series ends with Tom's wedding. |

- **Story timeline:** Beginner runs September 2025 – summer 2026, Intermediate runs September 2026 – July 2027, and Advanced runs September 2027 – July 2028.
- **What is invented:** the characters, Hartley's Tea, Edith Hartley, the Morita family, Crane Blend, the comic *Milk Second*, and all the companies and buildings in the story. Don't flag these as false. Do flag invented details that clash with real history or real places, for example a date, a law or a custom that couldn't be true.
- **What must be real:** every cultural, legal, historical and practical fact, including prices, laws, dates, customs, places and rules (NHS, renting, tax and National Insurance, visas, driving licences, Royal Mail, rail, weddings, etc.). Judge them as of 2026. Where a fact sits in a future story year (2027–28), flag it only if it's clearly wrong or likely to change.

## Deliberate choices (don't flag these as problems)

- Japanese characters only speak English to each other when a non-Japanese person is present.
- Mrs Hartley, the client, is deliberately indirect ("brave", "interesting", "I'll bear it in mind"). Decoding her is a running puzzle until Lesson 11.
- Josh's directness is his personality, not his nationality. Australian and British English are treated as essentially the same language.
- Teacher notes may use harder English than the student-facing slides.
- Grammar is not bolded in dialogues that come before that lesson's language focus slide. Vocabulary is bolded after the vocabulary slides.
- Each lesson has one role play, plus a progress check or final check where the unit ends.
- Practice slides that need a specific form start with a small pattern box (shown as *Pattern*).
- "British phrases" / "Useful phrases" slides come after the dialogues that use them.
- Slides marked "(flashback, black and white)" replay scenes the student has already seen.
- Lines like "[image: …]", "[video clip: …]" and "[audio: …]" are placeholders, and the description tells you what the student will see or hear. "(teacher only)" text is hidden from students.

## Already reviewed (don't re-raise unless you think the decision is wrong)

Earlier audits of each course have been applied. These points were considered and deliberately kept:

- Role plays give concrete details on the cards (times, prices, limits) so that teacher and student don't have to invent a situation. Freer speaking happens in the progress checks, the final checks and the discussion slides.
- Intermediate Lesson 11 (the reveal) is longer than other lessons and can be split into two sessions.
- Intermediate Lesson 2: Gwen's "You're supposed to see a flat before you pay" teaches *be supposed to* (what's expected), not a legal rule.
- The Intermediate mystery clue "American English from school" fits anyone who learned English in Japan (Mum, Kenta, Yuki). The suspect boards include "Someone else?" and "Someone in Japan?".
- Practical and legal information (renting, tax, pensions, licences) is deliberately simplified to what the student needs for the story situation, with fuller detail in teacher notes. Only flag it if it's wrong for that situation, not just incomplete.
- The series teaches speaking, listening and reading, not writing. Model emails and letters are for reading and phrase work.
- Practical facts were checked for 2026. Where the story is in 2027–28, the teacher notes say which year the figures are from.

This is the final audit of the **whole series**. Give extra weight to things a single-course review can't see: continuity between courses, progression of level from A2 to C1, characters' development, callbacks and repeated content across courses.

## What to check

### 1. Effectiveness at the target level

- Does each lesson actually teach its objective? Do the dialogues, language focus, practice, role play and wrap-up line up?
- Is the student-facing language right for the course level (A2 / B1–B2 / B2–C1)? Flag anything clearly above or below, using the CEFR Companion Volume and the British Council/EAQUALS Core Inventory.
- Is grammar taught in a sensible order across each course and across the series? Flag anything taught too early, too late, or re-taught without reason.
- Are practice items clear and answerable, and are the answers in the teacher notes correct and complete?
- Is earlier language recycled? Are there gaps in coverage for the level?

### 2. Story loopholes

- Does any character know something before the student has seen a scene where they learned it?
- Do events, dates, ages, places, relationships and objects stay consistent within each course and across all three?
- Is the mystery (Intermediate) and the archive puzzle (Advanced) fair play? Could a student solve them from what they've seen? Is any clue missing, contradicted or explained by something off-screen?
- Do the characters' reactions make sense? Is anything left unresolved that should have been resolved?

### 3. Unnecessary filler or confusion

- Flag slides, lines or activities that add nothing, repeat an earlier slide, or could confuse the student or teacher.
- Flag jokes that don't land without explanation, unclear instructions, and role plays where either person would have to invent a lot.

### 4. Cultural and historical accuracy

- List every factual claim you think is wrong, out of date or doubtful, and say why. Give a source if you can.
- Flag stereotypes (about British, Japanese, Australian, Welsh or Scottish people, or anyone else), even mild or friendly ones.
- Flag any British phrase that is dated, regional without explanation, American, or not really used today.
- Flag comparisons with Japan that are inaccurate or that tell the student what Japan is like instead of asking.

### 5. Tone and level appropriateness

- Would a real person say this line in this situation? Flag anything stiff, unnatural or machine-sounding.
- Do Haruka and Yuki sound like believable Japanese speakers of English at that stage, improving visibly across the series without becoming different people?
- Is the humour kind? Is anything inappropriate for adult learners or a professional setting?

### 6. Coherence across the series

- Do the three courses feel like one story, with consistent characters, running jokes, callbacks and progression?
- Does each course stand alone well enough for a new student, for example through "The story so far" slides?
- Does the ending of the series resolve the main threads?

### 7. Logical teaching order within each lesson

- Vocabulary is pre-taught before the dialogue that needs it.
- The language focus comes after the dialogues that contain its examples, and its "From the story" examples come from dialogues already read.
- Culture explanations come straight after the scene that raises them.
- Role plays come after the language they practise.
- The listening clips fit the story timeline.

## How to report

1. A table with one row per issue:

   | Course | Lesson | Slide | Quote | Problem | Category (1–7) | Severity (must fix / should fix / nice to have) | Suggested fix |

   Keep quotes short. In "Suggested fix", give the actual replacement line where you can.

2. The **10 most important issues** across the series, in order.
3. A short **verdict per course**: is it right for its level, and what is its biggest weakness?
4. **What works especially well**, briefly.
5. **Confidence:** for every factual claim you flag, say "sure" or "unsure". Don't guess. Say "unsure" and explain what to check.
