Follow Haruka from Osaka through her first year living in Britain. Learn everyday English for food, manners, weather, places, accents, music and travel, with real British culture.

### Slide 9: Story · Meet Tom

Upstairs in the **flat**, Gwen introduces Haruka to her flatmate, Tom.

**Gwen:** Haruka, this is Tom. He’s your **flatmate**.  
**Tom:** Alright? Nice to meet you, Haruka!  
**Haruka:** Nice to meet you too... “alright”? Am I OK?  
**Tom:** “Alright?” just means “Hi, how are you?”  
**Haruka:** Oh, I see! Where are you from, Tom?  
**Tom:** Manchester, in the north of England. And you?  
**Haruka:** I’m from Osaka. I’m a graphic designer.  

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 13: Pronunciation · Sounding natural

> **How it sounds**
>
> Nice to meet you → nice ta **MEE**-choo
>
> Where are you from? → wair-ra-ya **FROM**?
>
> How about you? → how a-bou-**CHOO**?

**Say it with your teacher**
- Nice to meet you, Tom.
- Nice to meet you **too**.
- Where are you **from**?
- How about **you**?

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Say each line, the student repeats. (~1 min)
The spelling on the slide shows how the words join in natural speech: “to” becomes a weak “ta”, and “t” + “you” becomes “choo” (meet you → mee-choo, about you → a-bou-choo). In southern English accents the r in “where” is only heard when the next word starts with a vowel: wair-ra.
Capitals show the stressed word. In “Nice to meet you too”, the stress moves to TOO. In “How about you?”, stress YOU.
This is for listening and copying, not spelling. Remind the student to write it the normal way.

</details>

### Slide 16: Reading · Sharing a flat in the UK

[image: Photo idea: young flatmates chatting and cooking in a small shared kitchen]

Many young people in the UK live in a **flat** with **flatmates**. Rent in big cities like London is very expensive, so sharing is cheaper. Flatmates usually have their own bedroom, but they share the kitchen, the bathroom and the living room.

Flatmates are not always friends at the start. Often, they meet for the first time on moving day! Some flatmates cook and eat together. Others just say hello in the kitchen.

Some British people wear shoes inside the house, but many take them off at the door. And when someone new arrives, British people often offer them a cup of tea.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. Correct pronunciation gently at the end of each paragraph, not mid-sentence. (~2.5 min)
Check: Why do people share flats? What do flatmates share? What do British people often offer a new person?

</details>

### Slide 20: Culture · Fish and chips on Friday

| Word | Type | Japanese | Meaning | Example |

| crisps | noun (plural) | ポテトチップス | thin potato snacks in a bag | A bag of **crisps**, please. |

> **Why Friday?**
>
> Lots of British people eat fish and chips on Friday. Why? 
> A long time ago, many Christians didn’t eat meat on Fridays, so they ate fish. Some people also say workers got paid on Friday, so they bought dinner at the chip shop.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 4: Story · Sunday lunch

Sunday, one o’clock. Haruka goes to **Gwen’s** flat. Tom is there too.

**Gwen:** Hello, love! Come in, come in.  
**Haruka:** Thank you, Mrs Davies. This is for you. It’s a small gift from Osaka.  
**Gwen:** Oh, how kind! But please, call me Gwen.  
**Haruka:** OK… Gwen! Do you cook a big lunch every Sunday?  
**Gwen:** Yes, every Sunday! It’s my favourite meal of the week.  
**Tom:** And I always help! Well… I sometimes help.  
**Gwen:** Tom never helps! He usually just eats.  

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Read once together (student = Haruka), then swap roles. (~2 min)

Haruka brings a gift (手土産), as many people do in Japan when they visit someone’s home. In the UK people often bring something small too, like flowers, chocolates or wine, so Gwen is happy.
“Mrs Davies”: Haruka is polite with an older person. Gwen prefers her first name. In the UK many people use first names quickly, even with older people.
Point out the frequency words: every Sunday, always, sometimes, never, usually.

### Slide 18: Reading · The Sunday roast

[image: Photo idea: Yorkshire puddings and roast potatoes with gravy]

### Slide 20: Reading · Sorry, sorry, sorry

### Slide 6: Culture · Why “lovely”?

[image: Photo idea: someone smiling and giving a thumbs-up in the rain]

Gwen said “Lovely weather, isn’t it?” on a rainy day. She didn’t mean it! This is **sarcasm**: saying the opposite of what you actually mean.

You’ll often hear people in Britain joke like this about small problems, like bad weather or a long queue. It’s friendly, not rude. The other person often joins in: “Oh yes, perfect weather for the beach!”

How can you tell? Look at the situation. If it’s raining and someone says “lovely”, they’re using sarcasm. Just smile, or make a joke back.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Background: Kate Fox (Watching the English) describes irony as a constant part of English conversation. Ask the student: do people in Japan joke like this? When?
Also: weather is a safe topic for small talk in Britain because it changes a lot and everyone can join in.

### Slide 16: Pronunciation · L and R

> **Two different sounds**
>
> **L**: your tongue touches behind your top teeth → **l**eft · **l**ine · p**l**atform
>
> **R**: your tongue doesn’t touch anything → **r**ight · **r**ed · t**r**ain
>
> **l**ight ≠ **r**ight · **l**ong ≠ w**r**ong

**Say it with your teacher**
- Turn **l**eft, then turn **r**ight.
- Take the Northern **l**ine.
- Go to p**l**atform two.
- Look, a **r**ed phone box!

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Let the student watch your mouth. Practise the pairs slowly, then fast. (~1 min)

</details>

### Slide 12: Pronunciation · Sentence rhythm

> **Strong words, quick words**
>
> Put stress on the important words: I’ve **DONE EV**erything on my **LIST**.
>
> Say the small words quickly without stress: I’ve, on, my, to, a, the.
>
> This makes your English sound natural and clear.

**Say it with your teacher**
- I’ve **DONE** **EV**erything on my **LIST**.
- I’ve de**CID**ed to **STAY**!
- We **CLIMBED** an **OLD** vol**CA**no.
- I’ll **COME** **HOME** for ** NEW YEAR**.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Clap the stressed words while you say each line; the small words fit between the claps. (~1 min)

Review: ask the student which pronunciation point from the course was the hardest, and practise it once more.

</details>
