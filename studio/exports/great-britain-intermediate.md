### Slide 13: Language focus · How long have you been…?

> **have / has been + -ing**
>
> *Still happening now*
>
> I**’ve been living** in London for a year. · She**’s been drawing** all day.
>
> *Asking how long*
>
> How long **have** you **been working** here? · **For** a year. / **Since** September.
>
> *Not with some verbs*
>
> I**’ve known** Tom for a year. (not “I’ve been knowing”)

**From the story**
- Priya: Dev**’s been using** that one for ten years.
- Yuki: How long **have** you **been working** here?
- Haruka: I**’ve been saving** this mug for you!

Use the present perfect continuous for something that started in the past and is still happening now.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Elicit the form from the examples first. Japanese learners often say “How long are you working here?” Correct it to “How long have you been working here?” (~2 min)
for + a period (for a year), since + a starting point (since September).
Verbs like know, like and have (= own) don’t usually use the continuous: “I’ve known Tom for a year.”

</details>

### Slide 19: Culture · Why is Tom so rude?

> **Teasing means we’re friends**
>
> In Britain, close friends often **tease** each other. They make jokes about each other, like Tom being late, and nobody gets angry.
>
> With people they don’t know well, many British people are very polite. So when a British friend starts teasing you, it can be a good sign: it often means “we’re close now”. People are different, though, and it’s fine to say if teasing feels too much.
>
> Many visitors find this strange at first, and some British people do too. You don’t have to tease back. “Very funny!” is a fine answer.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

This explains the joke in the last dialogue straight away. (~1.5 min)
Ask: Was Tom being rude? How would you feel if a friend teased you like that?
Teasing between friends is often called “banter”. The course comes back to banter later, including where the line is.

</details>

### Slide 26: Culture · What do they really mean?

> **Polite office English**
>
> At work, many people in Britain say things in a soft, indirect way. You might hear:
>
> “**Not bad**” = often means good!
> “**Quite good**” = can mean just OK.
> “**I’ll bear it in mind**” = often maybe… or maybe not.
> “**With respect…**” = often I don’t agree.
>
> Not sure what someone means? It’s fine to ask: “Sorry, do you mean…?”

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Office English that new people often misunderstand. (~1.5 min)
These are possible meanings, not fixed codes: tone and context matter. “Quite” can mean “fairly” (quite good = OK) or “very” (quite amazing), and American speakers use the “very” meaning more often. Yuki learned American English at school, so she might hear “quite good” as a big compliment.
Ask: Are there phrases in Japanese that mean something different from the words?

</details>

### Slide 4: Story · Too good to be true

Saturday morning. Yuki and Haruka look at flat adverts with Gwen.

**Yuki:** Look at this one. A big flat in Camden, only £900 a month!  
**Gwen:** £900? In Camden? That’s too good to be true, love.  
**Haruka:** The landlord is abroad. It says you need to send the deposit first, and he’ll post the keys.

### Slide 17: Reading · Renting in England

[image: Photo idea: new keys in front of a flat door]

Looking for a flat in England? Here are some things to check.

First, see the flat before you pay anything. Rental **scams** are common, and a very cheap flat with a landlord who is “abroad” is a warning sign.

Your **deposit** can be up to five weeks’ rent, and the landlord has to put it in a government scheme within 30 days. Letting agents can’t charge most fees, like admin fees.

The landlord will also check that you’re allowed to rent in England. If you have a visa, you give them a **share code**.

Finally, ask about **bills** and **council tax**. If you live alone, you get 25% off your council tax.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: What should you never do? How much can a deposit be? What’s a share code for?
Extra (teacher only): since 1 May 2026, under the Renters’ Rights Act, landlords in England can ask for no more than one month’s rent in advance, can’t accept bids above the advertised rent, and can’t use “no-fault” evictions.
Sources: GOV.UK and council guides on tenancy deposits (5-week cap, 30 days to protect), the Tenant Fees Act 2019, Right to Rent guidance (share codes), Action Fraud and Which? on rental scams, council guidance on the 25% single person discount.

</details>

### Slide 12: Language focus · Present perfect or past simple?

### Slide 20: Reading · Getting ill in the UK

In England, you can register with a **GP** (a family doctor) for free. You don’t need ID or proof of address, and you can do it online.

### Slide 18: Reading · Panto

[image: Photo idea: a panto dame on stage]

A **pantomime**, or **panto**, is a funny show for the whole family. Many British theatres put one on every winter, from late November to January.

Pantos tell famous children’s stories like Cinderella or Aladdin. The stories are simple, and the fun comes from the jokes. The **dame**, a funny woman in a crazy dress, is traditionally played by a man.

The audience is part of the show. People **boo** the villain, shout “Oh yes it is!” and warn the hero: “He’s behind you!”

Panto has roots in an Italian kind of theatre from the 1500s, mixed with British traditions. For many British children, it’s their first trip to the theatre.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: When can you see a panto? Who plays the dame? What do people shout?
Sources: History.co.uk and the University of Warwick on panto dames; Historic UK on pantomime; NPR (December 2025) on panto in Britain. Panto grew from commedia dell’arte, Italian theatre from the 1500s.

</details>

### Slide 12: Language focus · It had gone to Helsinki

> **Telling a story**
>
> *Background: past continuous*
>
> I **was waiting** at belt 5.
>
> *Main events: past simple*
>

### Slide 17: Reading · Banter

[image: Photo idea: friends laughing and teasing each other]

British friends often tease each other. It’s called **banter**: friendly jokes about small things, like always being late or being scared of a cat. Many British people see it as a sign of a close friendship.

For visitors, banter can be confusing. It can sound rude, and it’s hard to know when someone is joking. Some British people don’t like it either.

There is a line. Banter is usually between people who know each other well, and it’s about small, silly things, not someone’s body, family or background. If the other person isn’t laughing, it isn’t banter any more.

At work, be careful: unkind jokes can be bullying, even if someone says “It was just banter”.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Check: Why do friends tease each other? Why do visitors find banter hard? Where is the line?
Sources: Acas guidance lists unwanted jokes or banter among behaviours that can be bullying, and under the Equality Act 2010 offensive “banter” can be harassment. The rest describes a common British habit, using soft language (often, many).

### Slide 20: Reading · Late again?

[image: Photo idea: a rail replacement bus at a station]

British train tickets can be confusing. **Advance** tickets are the cheapest, but you can only use them on the train you booked. **Off-Peak** tickets are for quieter times of day, and **Anytime** tickets cost the most.

If your train is cancelled, your ticket is usually still valid on the next train or on a **rail replacement bus**.

And if you arrive late, you can often get money back. It’s called **Delay Repay**. With some train companies you can claim after 15 minutes; with others, after 30. The longer the delay, the more you get. After an hour, you usually get the full price of a single ticket back, and after two hours, the full price of a return.

Claim online within 28 days, and keep your ticket.

### Slide 12: Language focus · She said…, she told me…

> **Reporting**
>
> *say and tell*
>
> Priya **said** (that)… · Priya **told me** (that)… (not “said me”)

>

> *More examples*
>

### Slide 20: Reading · Dear, Hi or Hiya?

[image: Photo idea: writing an email on a laptop]

British emails can be more or less formal. To someone you don’t know, start with **Dear** and the name (Dear Ms Jones), and end with **Kind regards** or **Best wishes**.

With colleagues, most people write **Hi** and the first name, and end with **Thanks** or **Best**.

**Hiya** and **Cheers** are friendly and informal: fine for friends, but not for your first email to a new client.

Many British people end messages to friends and family with an **x**, a kiss. Between friends, it usually isn’t romantic. Some people use it with colleagues, but at work it’s safer not to.

Not sure? Copy the style of the other person’s email.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: How do you start an email to someone you don’t know? What does “x” mean? Should you use it at work?
This describes common British habits, using soft language (many, most). There are no fixed rules, which is why the last tip matters.

</details>

### Slide 14: Language focus · If I’d known…

> **If + had + past participle, would have + past participle**
>
> *It didn’t happen*
>
> If I**’d known**, I**’d have bought** champagne. (I didn’t know.)
>
> *A different result*
>
> If we**’d booked** directly, we**’d have had** breakfast.
>
> *Negative*
>
> If I **hadn’t stopped**, I **wouldn’t have lost** them.

**From the story**
- Tom: If I**’d known**, I**’d have bought** champagne.
- Tom: If we**’d booked** directly, we**’d have had** breakfast included!
- Haruka: If I **hadn’t stopped** for a photo, I **wouldn’t have lost** them!

Use the third conditional to imagine a different past.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Third conditional: an imaginary past. It didn’t happen, and we imagine a different result. If + past perfect, would have + past participle. Often used for regrets and jokes. (~2 min)
Mixed conditionals (If I’d booked directly, I’d be happy now) are for recognition only at this level.

</details>

### Slide 18: Reading · Beside the seaside

[image: Photo idea: deckchairs on a pebble beach]

In 1871, a law created the UK’s first **bank holidays**. For many working people, it meant a free day for the first time, and new railways could take them to the sea for a cheap day out.

Seaside towns like Brighton and Blackpool grew fast. They built long **piers** with theatres, cafés and games. Brighton Palace Pier opened in 1899, and it’s still there today.

A British seaside day often means fish and chips, ice cream, the arcades and a walk along the pier, even when it’s windy. And Brighton’s beach isn’t sand: it’s **pebbles**!

Sources: Bank Holidays Act 1871 (Sir John Lubbock); History of Parliament on Victorian MPs and holidays; Brighton Palace Pier history (opened 20 May 1899).

### Slide 4: Story · On the bus
