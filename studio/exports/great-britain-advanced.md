### Slide 10: Story · The meeting

Ten o’clock. Haruka chairs the meeting. Josh and Yuki.

**Haruka:** Right, first **item**: Yuki’s new design for the bakery client. Yuki?  
**Yuki:** So, this is the latest version. I’ve changed the colours since last week.  
**Josh:** Honestly? It doesn’t work. The logo’s too small, and those colours are boring.  
**Yuki:** Boring? I’d say they’re slightly more… calm.  
**Haruka:** Thanks, Josh. I wonder if we could hear a bit more from Yuki first?  
**Josh:** Sure. But it still doesn’t work.  
**Haruka:** OK. Let’s put a pin in that and come back to it after lunch.  

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 11: Culture · Direct or soft?

> **How people disagree at work**
>
> In many British workplaces, people **soften** criticism. Instead of “That’s wrong”, you might hear “I’m not sure that quite works” or “Could we look at that again?”.
>
> Other people, like Josh, prefer to say exactly what they think. Neither style is wrong, but when they meet, people can **come across** badly without meaning to.
>
> In a meeting, softening can help: people often listen more when they don’t feel attacked. Styles vary a lot between workplaces and people.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

This explains the clash in the meeting. (~1.5 min)
Styles differ between people and workplaces, not only countries. Don’t let the student generalise about any nationality.
Ask: In Japan, how do people disagree in a meeting?

</details>

### Slide 13: Language focus · Softening

> **Softening and hedging**
>
> *Soft openers*
>
> **I’m not sure** that works. · **I wonder if** we could…
>
> *Small words*
>
> It’s **a bit** small. · It’s **slightly** dark. · It’s not **quite** right. · **I’d say** it’s calm.
>
> *Questions instead of statements*
>
> **Could we** try a bigger logo? · **Would it be worth** changing the colours?

**From the story**
- Haruka: **I’m not sure** I’m **quite** ready.
- Yuki: **I’d say** they’re **slightly** more… calm.
- Haruka: **I wonder if** we could hear a bit more from Yuki?

Use softening language to disagree or give feedback without sounding rude.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Softening and hedging make disagreement easier to hear. Main tools: modal verbs (might, could), “I wonder if…”, “I’m not sure…”, small words (a bit, slightly, quite), and “I’d say…”. Some learners have many politeness strategies in Japanese but fewer ready-made ones in English, so they can sound blunter than they mean to. (~2 min)

</details>

### Slide 17: Pronunciation · Soft, not flat

> **Fall-rise for softening**
>
> I’m not **SURE** ↘↗ that works. (soft, open to discussion)
> That doesn’t **WORK**. ↘ (final)

**Say it with your teacher**
- I’m not **sure** ↘↗ that works.
- It’s **slightly** ↘↗ dark.
- **Could** we ↘↗ try again?
- I’d **say** ↘↗ it’s calm.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Softening works best with a fall-rise on the key word: the voice goes down and then up a little, as if to say “…but I could be wrong”. A flat or falling voice sounds more final. Say each line; the student repeats. (~1.5 min)

</details>

### Slide 19: Reading · How to run a good meeting

[image: Photo idea: a meeting table with an agenda and sticky notes]

A good meeting starts before anyone walks into the room. Send the **agenda** a day or two before, so people can prepare.

Start on time and finish on time. If the chair waits for latecomers, everyone learns that being late is fine.

A good chair makes sure quiet people get a turn and stops one person taking over. Ideas that are off-topic get “parked” for later.

Finish with **action points**: who will do what, and by when. Then send short notes the same day.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: What should people get before the meeting? What happens to off-topic ideas? What should every action point have?
This is general good practice; workplaces vary.

</details>

### Slide 12: Language focus · She suggested…

> **Reporting verbs**
>
> *verb + -ing*
>

>

### Slide 6: Culture · What’s on a UK payslip?

> **Your payslip, line by line**
>
> **Gross pay:** your pay before anything is taken off.
>
> **Income tax:** most people pay no tax on their first £12,570 a year. The tax code **1257L** shows this. After that, the basic rate is 20%.
>
> **National Insurance (NI):** 8% of most of what you earn over £12,570. It helps pay for the State Pension and some benefits.
>
> **Pension:** if you’re eligible, you’re put into a workplace pension automatically.
>
> **Net pay:** what you take home.
>
> **0T** means no tax-free pay at all. Employers often use it when they don’t have a new worker’s details yet.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Explains the system straight after the scene. Figures are for England, Wales and Northern Ireland, checked for 2026/27 (Scotland has its own income tax rates; NI is the same across the UK). The story is in 2027/28: the £12,570 allowance and NI thresholds are frozen until April 2031, but check the rates before teaching. (~2 min)
New starters give their employer a P45 from their last UK job or fill in HMRC’s starter checklist. With no information, the employer uses 0T.
Ask: What’s taken off your pay in Japan? Who works out your tax?
Sources: GOV.UK income tax rates and Personal Allowance (£12,570, frozen); GOV.UK National Insurance rates 2026/27 (8% between £12,570 and £50,270, 2% above); Autumn Budget 2025 (thresholds frozen until April 2031); HMRC starter checklist guidance (0T when no information is given).

### Slide 18: Reading · Beating jet lag

[image: Photo idea: morning light after a long flight]

**Jet lag** happens when your body clock doesn’t match the time where you are. You might feel tired in the day, wide awake at night, or hungry at strange times.

Flying east, from London to Tokyo for example, is usually harder than flying west. Your day gets shorter, and most body clocks find it easier to stay up late than to go to bed early.

Before you go, change your sleep times a little. On the plane, drink plenty of water and go easy on alcohol and coffee. When you arrive, get outside in the morning light and eat at local times.

Most people feel normal again within a few days.

### Slide 11: Language focus · What they loved was…

> **Cleft sentences**
>
> *What + clause + is / was…*
>
> **What they loved was** the history. · **What Josh means is** (that)…
>
> *It + is / was + focus + that / who…*
>
> **It’s the price that’s** the problem. · **It was** Haruka **who** found the answer.
>
> *To correct someone*
>
> **It isn’t** the tea, **it’s** the price.

**From the story**
- Haruka: **What they really loved was** the history.
- Haruka: **What Josh means is** that your customers might value the history.
- Ms Kato: **It’s the price that’s** the sticking point, not the tea.

Use cleft sentences to focus on the most important part, or to clarify and correct.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Cleft sentences split one idea into two parts to put the focus on the important part. What-clefts (What I mean is…) are great for clarifying; it-clefts (It’s the price that…) are great for correcting (It’s the price, not the tea). They’re C1-level tools for meetings and mediation. (~2.5 min)
Many Japanese learners show emphasis with word order or particles (は / が); in English, clefts and stress do the same job.

</details>


### Slide 12: Practice · Make it clear

> *Pattern*
>
> What + … + is / was …
> It’s / It was + focus + that / who …

- They loved the old tins. (What…)
- The price worries Ms Kato. (It’s…)
- Josh needs more patience. (What…)
- Haruka booked the hotel, not Josh. (It was…)
- I mean we need more time. (What I mean…)
- We didn’t like the colour. The design was fine. (It was…)

> **Answers** (teacher only)
>
> 1. What they loved was the old tins.
> 2. It’s the price that worries Ms Kato.
> 3. What Josh needs is more patience.
> 4. It was Haruka who booked the hotel, not Josh.
> 5. What I mean is (that) we need more time.

### Slide 17: Reading · Is royal milk tea royal?

[image: Photo idea: milk tea in a Tokyo café]

Ask for a “royal milk tea” in London, and you’ll probably get a confused look. The name comes from Japan. The Lipton tea house in Kyoto says it was the first to serve it, in 1965.

Royal milk tea is made by brewing black tea in milk. In Britain, people usually make tea with water and add a little milk.

Black tea is popular in Japan in other ways too. Kirin’s Gogo no Kocha (“Afternoon Tea”), launched in 1986, is one of the country’s best-known bottled teas.

So a British tea company in Japan isn’t starting from zero.

### Slide 13: Language focus · If you’d told me…

> **Mixed conditionals**
>
> *Past → now* (If + had + past participle, would + verb)
>
> **If you’d told** me on Monday, I **wouldn’t be** behind now.
>
> *Always / now → past* (If + past simple, would have + past participle)
>
> **If I were** more patient, I**’d have waited**.

**From the story**
- Josh: **If you’d told** me on Monday, I **wouldn’t be** this far behind now.
- Josh: **If I were** more patient, I**’d have waited**.
- Josh: **If you were** more direct, we**’d have saved** a week.

Use mixed conditionals when the condition and the result are at different times.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Mixed conditionals mix time. Past condition → present result: If you’d told me on Monday (past), I wouldn’t be behind now (present). Present or general condition → past result: If I were more patient (always), I’d have waited (that day). The second and third conditionals were taught in the intermediate course; this lesson only mixes them. (~2.5 min)
Many Japanese learners keep both halves in the same time. Ask: Is this part about the past, or now?

</details>

### Slide 20: Reading · Feedback that helps

[image: Photo idea: two colleagues talking over coffee]

Good feedback is specific. “Great job!” feels nice but doesn’t help anyone improve, and “You’re always late!” starts an argument.

Many managers use a simple model called **SBI**: Situation, Behaviour, Impact. “In Monday’s meeting, you interrupted Yuki twice, and she stopped sharing ideas.”

Give feedback soon and in private. Then stop talking, and ask how the other person sees it.

When you receive feedback, try not to defend yourself straight away. Say thank you, ask questions, and take time to think. If you disagree, you can still say: “Thanks. I see it differently, and here’s why.”

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: What does SBI stand for? When and where should you give feedback? What should you do when you receive it?
Sources: Center for Creative Leadership, “Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) feedback model”. The other tips are common workplace practice.

</details>

### Slide 17: Reading · Tea in wartime

[image: Photo idea: an old teapot and a ration book]

During the Second World War, the British government controlled the country’s tea, because almost all of it came from abroad by ship.

In July 1940, tea was **rationed**. Each adult could have about 2 ounces (57 grams) a week, enough for around three weak cups a day.

Tea was seen as important for people’s spirits, and the government made sure there was always some.

Rationing continued long after the war. Tea was finally taken off the ration in October 1952.

### Slide 11: Language focus · Written in 1923, …

> **Participle clauses**
>
> *Past participle (passive meaning)*
>
> **Written** in 1923, the letter describes the tea garden.
>
> *-ing (active, often at the same time)*
>
> **Looking** through the box, Ruth found one more letter.
>
> *Having + past participle (one thing, then another)*
>
> **Having read** the letter, Josh felt terrible.

**From the story**
- Haruka: **Written** in 1923, this letter is from two years before…
- Ruth: **Looking** through this box, I found one more.
- Josh: **Having read** that, I feel terrible about my neon pink.

Use participle clauses to make summaries shorter and more formal.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Participle clauses make writing and summaries shorter and more formal. -ed participle = passive meaning (Written in 1923 = The letter was written in 1923). -ing = active, often at the same time. Having + past participle = one action finished before the next. The subject of both parts must be the same: “Having read that, I feel…” (I read it, I feel). (~2.5 min)
Many Japanese learners join clauses with and / so. Participle clauses are a C1 tool for summaries and presentations.

</details>

### Slide 11: Language focus · Not only has…

> **Inversion for emphasis**
>
> *Not only + auxiliary + subject…, but…*
>
> **Not only has** the damage got worse, **but**…
>
> *Negative start + auxiliary + subject*
>
> **At no point have** you replied. · **Never have** I seen this.
>
> *Should + subject (= If)*
>
> **Should** the problem **continue**, I will…

**From the story**
- Letter: **Not only has** the damage deteriorated, **but**…
- Letter: **At no point have** you arranged for anyone to inspect it.
- Letter: **Should** the problem **continue**, I will contact the council.

Use inversion to sound formal and strong, especially in writing.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

Inversion for emphasis: after a negative or limiting expression at the start (Not only, At no point, Never, Under no circumstances), the auxiliary comes before the subject, like a question. It sounds formal and strong, so it’s common in complaints, speeches and news. “Should + subject + verb” is a formal way to say “If”. (~2.5 min)
Many Japanese learners forget the auxiliary (Not only the damage has…). Point at the question order: has the damage → Not only has the damage…

</details>


### Slide 12: Practice · Make it stronger

> *Pattern*
>
> Not only + is / has + subject…, but…
> At no point / Never + have + subject…
> Should + subject + verb, …

- The leak is dangerous, and it’s getting worse. (Not only…)
- You have never replied to my emails. (At no point…)
- If you have any questions, please contact me. (Should…)
- I have never seen such a slow response. (Never…)
- The heating is broken, and the windows don’t close. (Not only…)

> **Answers** (teacher only)
>
> 1. Not only is the leak dangerous, but it’s getting worse.
> 2. At no point have you replied to my emails.
> 3. Should you have any questions, please contact me.
> 4. Never have I seen such a slow response.
> 5. Not only is the heating broken, but the windows don’t close either.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student rewrites each sentence with inversion, starting with the words in brackets. (~2 min)

</details>

### Slide 10: Language focus · First, I’ll…

> **Signposting**
>
> *Structure*
>
> **First**, I’ll… · **Then** I’ll **move on to**… · **Finally**, …
>
> *Changing topic*
>
> **Moving on to**… · **That brings me to**…
>

>

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 17: Reading · Less is more

### Slide 9: Language focus · If you could…, we’d…

> **Negotiating**
>

> *Conditions*

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 16: Reading · Getting to yes

[image: Photo idea: two people working out a deal]

In 1981, Roger Fisher and William Ury wrote **Getting to Yes**, one of the most famous books on negotiating.

One of their ideas is your **BATNA**: your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. In other words, what will you do if there’s no deal? If you know your BATNA, you know when to walk away.

They also say: focus on **interests**, not positions. Mr Bell’s position was “too expensive”. His interest was not losing money. Paying half later solved that.

The best deals leave both sides feeling they’ve won.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

### Slide 10: Culture · British weddings

> **A wedding day in Britain**
>
> British weddings can take place in a church, a register office or a licensed venue such as a hotel or a barn.
>
> After the ceremony comes the **reception**: a meal, **speeches** and dancing. Traditionally, the father of the bride, the groom and the best man give speeches. Today, brides, mothers and friends often speak too.
>
> The best man’s speech is famous for gently embarrassing the groom. Speeches often end with a **toast**: guests raise their glasses and say the couple’s names.

### Slide 13: Language focus · What I didn’t know was…

> **Emphasis in speeches**
>
> *Cleft sentences*
>
> **What I didn’t know was** that… · **It was** you **who**…
>
> *Inversion*
>
> **Never have I seen** him so smart. · **Not only is** he funny, **but**…
>
> *Emphatic do*
>
> He **does** always turn up. · I **do** hope you’ll visit.

**From the story**
- Haruka: **What I didn’t know was** that I’d just met my best friend.
- Haruka: **Never have I seen** him look so smart.
- Haruka: He **does**, however, always turn up.

Use these structures to make a speech memorable.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

A review of three ways to add emphasis, perfect for speeches. Cleft sentences (Lesson 5) put the focus on one idea. Inversion (Lesson 9) sounds dramatic. Emphatic do (new) stresses that something really is true, often after a contrast: He’s always late. He does, however, always turn up. Stress the do / does. (~2.5 min)
Many Japanese learners use “really” or “very” for emphasis. These structures make a speech sound more natural and more memorable.

</details>

### Slide 18: Reading · Something old, something new

[image: Photo idea: a wedding cake and confetti]

Many British brides follow an old rhyme: “Something old, something new, something borrowed, something blue.” The old item connects the bride to her family’s past, and the new one to her future.

After the ceremony, guests often throw **confetti**, small pieces of paper or dried petals, for good luck.

The cake is cut together by the couple. Traditionally it was a fruit cake, but sponge cakes, cheese towers and even piles of doughnuts are popular now.

Like Haruka’s story, a good wedding mixes something old with something new.

<details><summary>Teacher notes</summary>

The student reads aloud. (~2.5 min)
Check: What does the old rhyme say? Why do people throw confetti?
These are common traditions, not rules; many couples choose their own. The rhyme is Victorian (often linked to Lancashire).
